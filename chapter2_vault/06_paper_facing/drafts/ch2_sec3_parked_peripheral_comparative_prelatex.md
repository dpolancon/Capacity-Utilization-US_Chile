## 1. Peripheral Divided Management & The Non-Reinvestment Paradox

A firm attempting to industrialize in the periphery faces a structural contradiction: it needs advanced machinery to remain competitive, but that machinery cannot be produced domestically. Industrialization through import substitution (ISI) creates the appearance of autarky—domestic production replacing imported consumer goods—without reducing dependence on external capital goods sources. Instead, ISI relocates that dependence: instead of importing finished consumer goods, the periphery imports the means of production ($K^{ME}$).

The foreign exchange required to purchase imported machinery oscillates with commodity cycles and world prices beyond peripheral control. This structural dependence generates a **non-reinvestment problem**: a peripheral firm cannot automatically reinvest accumulated accounting profit into expanded capacity, because equipment imports depend on foreign exchange availability and state allocation, which are externally determined.

The firm faces a choice:
1. Reinvest strictly at the pace allowed by the foreign-exchange import cycle, or
2. Accumulate surplus in liquid, financial forms (cash reserves, dollar-denominated assets) held as hedges against moments when capital goods imports become accessible.

This non-reinvestment of surplus replicates Vidal's (2022) divided-management contradiction inside the peripheral firm. Management cannot present to its workforce a coherent long-term accumulation strategy. Management must simultaneously demand wage suppression and labor intensification (cost containment under structural constraint) while lacking the physical capital goods required to justify worker cooperation or deliver productivity expansion. Labor responds by withholding the discretionary effort and tacit problem-solving that management cannot coherently claim.

The balance of payments (BoP) thus becomes the medium through which external structural constraint channels into internal workplace pressure:
```
Foreign Exchange Scarcity 
  ↓
Import Restriction on Capital Goods & Parts
  ↓
Underutilization of Installed Capacity
  ↓
Wage Suppression & Cost-Containment Pressure
  ↓
Labor Resistance & Workplace Non-Cooperation
  ↓
Fractured Operational Utilization Settlement
```

The peripheral firm's capacity utilization configuration is therefore the institutional settlement through which it manages not an abstract contradiction between markets and planning, but the concrete contradiction between technological dependence and financial constraint imposed by its structural position in the world capitalist division of labor.

## 2. State-Mediated Capacity Settlements under Import Substitution

The capacity utilization configurations that emerge from peripheral structural positions are not timeless; they are historically specific settlements shaped by how particular state forms attempt to mediate the contradiction between technological dependence and financial constraint.

Import substitution as a development strategy took a specific institutional form in the 1950s and 1960s:
- Tariff protection for domestic consumer-goods industries
- State credit allocation toward capital goods imports
- Exchange controls directing foreign exchange toward prioritized sectors
- Institutional instruments including development corporations (e.g., CORFO in Chile), central banks, and directed credit systems

The developmental state attempted to compensate for the market's structural inability to coordinate capital-goods allocation by directly managing foreign exchange and capital-goods flows. This settlement operated through differential integration into the world market: the core maintained direct control over technological innovation and capital-goods production, while the periphery specialized in primary commodity exports and import-substituting industries consuming core technology.

Because the terms of trade systematically favored the core (Prebisch 1981), capital-goods prices rose relative to primary commodity prices. This created **technological dependency**: the periphery was forced to continuously export more primary commodities to import equivalent physical machinery, yet the foreign-exchange constraint never disappeared. Development appeared as an impossible chase: accumulate faster, export more, import more machinery, increase productivity—yet never escape the external constraint that defined peripheral position.

The feasibility of such settlements depended on strict historical conditions:
1. Sufficient commodity export revenues to generate foreign exchange
2. State capacity to enforce exchange controls and credit rationing
3. Labor organization that could be corporatist-incorporated rather than violently suppressed
4. Cold War geopolitical contexts that tolerated developmental state experimentation

Peripheral capacity utilization configurations are the institutional expressions through which developmental states attempted to mediate contradictions arising from peripheral position within a capitalist world economy where neither firm-level optimization nor market-coordinated adjustment was available.

## 3. Comparative-Relational Crisis Entanglement

The general framework—market anarchy in its value form, divided management as its form of appearance inside the firm, and contradictory utilization configurations as the institutional settlement—is not specific to peripheral capitalism. It is the general framework through which capitalist accumulation operates across all structural nodes.

What is specific to peripheral capitalism is the *configuration*: the particular conditions under which contradictions are mediated, the constraints on what mediation is possible, and the institutional forms that settlements take.

This distinction is crucial for comparative political economy. The crisis of Atlantic Fordism cannot be analyzed as a national event in the United States and Western Europe whose effects then propagated outward to a periphery operating on a different logic. The core and periphery were not operating on different logics. They operated on the **same logic**—the same anarchy of decentralized accumulation, the same divided management, the same labor resistance—under different structural conditions, producing different historical settlements.

When those settlements broke down in the 1970s, they broke down together because their stabilizing conditions were mutually entangled:
- **Core stabilization configurations:** Dependent on military expenditure as a permanent demand component, credit-financed mass consumption, and U.S. dollar hegemony. These depended on peripheral configurations that supplied strategic raw materials at administered prices, absorbed core capital-goods exports through protected markets, and recycled surpluses through financial channels controlled by the core.
- **Peripheral stabilization configurations:** Dependent on import substitution, state-directed credit, and FX controls. These depended on core growth that generated steady demand for peripheral primary exports.

A comparative-relational analysis of crisis takes this entanglement as its starting point.

## 4. Analytical Hinge to Transformation Elasticity ($\theta_{periph}$)

What is at stake in the peripheral transformation elasticity $\theta_{periph}$ is not a static parameter to be estimated. It is the institutional mediation through which capital accumulation is converted into productive capacity across differentiated structural positions in the world capitalist system.

In Section 3.3 and Section 4.2, this formulation will be operationalized through:
1. The FX-conversion bottleneck parameter $\phi_t = \min(1, F_t^{avail}/F_t^{req})$
2. The effective profit share squeeze $\pi^{eff}_t = \pi_t \cdot \phi_t$
3. The peripheral capacity penalty $\kappa(\phi_t)$ measuring maintenance backlogs and unutilized imported capacity
4. Cointegration specification $D_{periph}$ linking Chilean capital stocks to foreign-exchange realization gates
