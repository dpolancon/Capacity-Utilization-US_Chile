# Section 2: The Micro-Meso Hinge: Okishio, Vidal, and Capacity Formation

Chapter 1 established that the macroeconomic capacity ceiling is not a neutral engineering constraint, but a historically contingent outcome conditioned by the balance of class power. Yet, identifying this distributional conditioning at the macro level leaves the underlying transmission mechanism black-boxed. How, precisely, does distributive conflict physically alter the conversion of accumulated capital into productive capacity?

Answering this requires dismantling a hidden ontological premise of heterodox growth models: the unified firm. Whether treating the normal rate of capacity utilization ($\mu_t \equiv Y_t / Y^p_t$, where $Y_t$ is realized output and $Y^p_t$ is potential capacity) as an endogenous behavioral target or an exogenous gravitational anchor, standard frameworks presume a coherent organization capable of selecting a benchmark, enforcing it across the labor process, and sustaining it over time. 

This premise collapses when confronted with the social organization of capitalist production. In a decentralized capitalist economy, individual capitals commit fixed investments without *ex-ante* coordination, confronting one another in competitive markets. Within the enterprise, production decisions encounter contradictory managerial and worker imperatives. Capacity utilization cannot be reduced to a target selected by a single optimizing entity. To theorize the political economy of productive capacity, we must replace the unified firm with two interconnected frameworks: Okishio’s theory of decentralized market anarchy, and Vidal’s theory of the internally divided firm.

## 2.1 Smooth Reproduction and the Unified-Firm Premise

Smooth capitalist reproduction requires strict material proportionality between departments of production, a condition formally proven by Basu (2022) and defined by Okishio (2022) as the equilibrium accumulation trajectory. Bergmann et al. (2009) refer to this balanced trajectory—where realized output meets productive capacity ($\mu_t = 1$)—as the "golden path." However, this proportional benchmark serves strictly as an analytical condition of feasibility, not a historical tendency or a gravitational attractor. Indeed, while a utilization rate of unity ($\mu_t = 1$) is a necessary condition for balanced reproduction, it is not sufficient; full macroeconomic reproduction also requires that the rate of accumulation matches the growth rate of the labor force and technical change. Nothing in the logic of decentralized accumulation establishes that competition will reproduce these proportions or pull the macroeconomy back toward unity.

Because private investment decisions are made without *ex-ante* coordination, real capitalist economies routinely violate the strict conditions of the golden path. Okishio (2022) demonstrates that this chronic disproportionality is not a temporary friction but the structural norm of decentralized accumulation. Treating this transient benchmark as a behavioral target relies on the flawed premise of the unified firm.

In reality, the denominator of the utilization ratio (productive capacity $Y^p_t$) is not an engineering constant, but a contested settlement of divided management and shop-floor struggle; the numerator (realized output $Y_t$) is similarly hostage to the anarchy of demand. Smooth reproduction supplies a reference case against which this historical non-coincidence can be studied; it does not describe the path capitalist accumulation actually follows.

## 2.2 Decentralized Accumulation and Market Anarchy

If the “golden path” of smooth reproduction is merely a benchmark of feasibility, the actual trajectory of capitalist accumulation is governed by the decentralized organization of investment. Capitalist production is coordinated through exchange, meaning that the social validation of private decisions occurs only *ex-post*. Because money mediates exchange, sale and purchase are separated moments of the capital circuit (Basu, 2022). This constitutes the logic of capital as impersonal domination: capitalists make their production and investment decisions with the law of value operating _“behind their backs”_ [^marx_behind_backs]. They are subject to market forces and value relations they neither control nor consciously direct. Okishio (2022) locates the source of macroeconomic instability precisely in this structural anarchy. Individual capitals commit to fixed investment on the basis of private profit expectations, yet the market validates those commitments only after production has begun. No firm can know in advance whether its expansion fits the investment and consumption decisions taken elsewhere. The coordination problem is not one of imperfect information surrounding an otherwise coherent plan; no economy-wide plan exists.

[^marx_behind_backs]: Karl Marx, *Capital*, Volume I, Chapter 1, Section 4 ("The Fetishism of Commodities and the Secret thereof"), where he describes the law of value operating as an overriding law of nature when private producers exchange products.

Smooth reproduction requires proportional expansion between Department I, which produces means of production, and Department II, which produces consumption goods. Because private investment does not enforce this proportionality, firms extrapolate past prices, profit rates, and sales. A signal that induces expansion in one branch routinely produces excess capacity before complementary demand materializes elsewhere. Contraction works through the same cumulative mechanism: falling sales cut investment, reducing aggregate demand and validating further cuts. Prices register the resulting imbalance, but they do not prevent the sunk commitments that produced it. 

Fixed capital gives this temporal lag its structural force. Structures and machinery embody long-horizon commitments that cannot be reversed when relative prices or demand conditions shift. Once installed, an unvalidated project leaves the firm to operate below its anticipated scale, defer maintenance, hold equipment idle, or devalue the asset. These responses distribute the costs of market anarchy among firms, workers, and creditors, but they do not restore the missing *ex-ante* coordination. 

Idle capacity is therefore a recurrent, structural form of decentralized accumulation rather than a temporary friction. Private investment physically builds capacity before the market can validate its social use, repeatedly generating a gap between installed productive potential ($Y^p_t$) and realized output ($Y_t$). In this framework, the capacity utilization rate ($\mu_t$) operates as the macroeconomic "exhaust valve" for structural disproportionality. Rather than adjusting investment to smoothly restore the golden path of balanced growth, the system absorbs the shock of uncoordinated accumulation through fluctuating utilization rates. The condition $\mu_t = 1$ is therefore at most a transient point of accidental proportionality, while persistent divergence records the ongoing reality of market anarchy. 

Yet Okishio’s macro framework leaves a crucial gap: it does not explain how firms translate this external anarchy into internal operating decisions. A firm does not receive a price signal and respond as a frictionless, unified unit. It processes the signal through an organization divided over control, cooperation, effort, and the allocation of resources. Vidal’s internally divided firm provides that missing mediation.

## 2.3 The Internally Divided Firm

Vidal reconstructs Marx’s Volume I theory of the labor process for the contemporary organizational era. Marx established that capitalist management faces a constitutive contradiction: it must simultaneously organize the cooperative labor required for production and enforce the coercive control required for valorization (surplus extraction). Vidal demonstrates how this contradiction fractures the modern capitalist firm, treating it as a divided social organization rather than a single-minded production function (Vidal, 2013, 2022). Management must extract surplus value through control over labor, but production also requires workers’ cooperation, tacit knowledge, and problem-solving capacity. Standardization and surveillance secure a measurable pace of work while weakening the discretion needed to sustain complex production. The firm cannot maximize both imperatives at once because the labor process that produces value is also the site where workers contest its extraction.

Workers respond to this contradiction by actively calibrating effort, cooperation, machine pacing, and the deployment of tacit knowledge. Capitalist management extracts potential capacity through concrete shop-floor mechanisms: increasing machine speed (speedups), deferring maintenance to hit short-term throughput targets, and enforcing rigid surveillance. Workers contest this extraction through labor turnover, collective exit, or the deliberate withdrawal of problem-solving cooperation and effort. Throughput, quality, and maintenance therefore depend on social relations that no engineering specification fixes in advance. A production target that raises machine speed by deferring maintenance may increase current output while degrading sustainable capacity. A directive that suppresses worker discretion may meet a short-term labor-control objective while worsening workflow bottlenecks. Labor effort is consequently not a fixed technical parameter applied to a pre-existing capital stock; it is an active, contested determinant of the capacity denominator itself.

This dynamic enforces a strict wedge between *engineering capacity* and *practical capacity*. Engineering capacity records the theoretical maximum output of equipment under ideal technical conditions. Practical capacity records the sustainable output rate dictated by actual maintenance schedules, product-mix complexity, and the negotiated intensity of labor. When managers report capacity to national statistical agencies, they rely on conventions shaped by their organizational position and the immediate pressures of the shop floor. Aggregate capacity statistics therefore do not reveal a neutral physical ceiling; they aggregate situated, institutional judgments. For the purposes of macroeconomic modeling, the productive capacity ceiling ($Y^p_t$) is not an exogenous engineering constraint, but an endogenous settlement of workplace conflict.

Because the valorization-labor process contradiction cannot be solved, firms settle into organizational configurations of "mediocre sufficiency" (Vidal, 2022). These are hybrid arrangements that reproduce production without resolving the underlying conflict between control and cooperation. Partial worker autonomy coexists with rigid discipline; continuous improvement programs coexist with brutal throughput pressure; idle machinery coexists with binding bottlenecks. These arrangements are not temporary frictions or departures from an otherwise unified, optimizing firm. They are the permanent, structural forms through which capitalist management physically converts accumulated capital into usable capacity.

## 2.4 Distribution, Technique, and the Transformation of Accumulation

The choice of technique connects the divided firm to distribution, governing how accumulated capital is physically transformed into productive capacity. Capitalists adopt a new technique when it reduces costs at prevailing prices and real wages. Okishio’s theorem shows that a viable capital-using, labor-saving (CU-LS) technique raises the profit rate when the real wage remains fixed (Okishio, 1961). Marx’s account allows the same form of technical change to depress profitability when rising capital intensity and the distribution of productivity gains increase the capital advanced relative to surplus value. The Marx–Okishio threshold identifies the rate of real-wage growth that separates these outcomes (Basu, 2022). It is a distributive boundary set by the balance of class forces, not a technical constant.

This boundary gives wage pressure a precise role in mechanization. When organized labor compels real wages to follow productivity, labor-saving techniques become more attractive while workers retain part of the resulting productivity gain. Under the institutional settlement of Atlantic Fordism—what Basu (2026) formalizes as the "strong labor" closure—this mechanism joined rapid mechanization to continuing conflict over profitability (Vidal, 2015, 2019). Wage-led mechanization was not a harmonious productivity bargain; the same distributive pressure that induced technical change contested capital’s claim on its proceeds.

The weakening of organized labor after the Fordist crisis altered that mechanism. Under the "weak labor" Post-Fordism, wage suppression reduced the distributive pressure to replace labor, even as lower relative machinery prices and technical change in capital-goods production continued to favor mechanization. Distribution and machinery prices therefore constitute a **dual-channel mechanism** in the choice of technique. Their relative force changes across accumulation regimes, which prevents the wage share from being treated as an external control added to an otherwise autonomous technical relation.

These channels still operate through the contradictions identified above. Firms choose techniques before demand validates the associated capacity, and they operate those techniques through labor processes that divide control from productive cooperation. Investment consequently does not translate one-for-one into a stable productive ceiling. The transformation depends on the composition of capital, the distributive conditions governing mechanization, and the organization through which installed equipment becomes usable capacity.

Okishio and Vidal thus rule out a direct passage from accumulation to productive capacity. Decentralized investment generates disproportionality across firms and sectors; the internally divided firm transmits that disproportionality into contested operating conditions. The relevant object is therefore the aggregate **transformation elasticity ($\theta \equiv d y^p / d k$)**, defined in log-levels to ensure a dimensionless parameter, which governs how the accumulation of capital translates into potential capacity expansion under distribution and market anarchy.

## 2.5 Historical Regimes of Capacity Formation in the Center and the Periphery

The theoretical framework developed above—combining Okishio’s market anarchy, Vidal’s divided firm, and the Marx-Okishio threshold—provides the political economy foundation for understanding how the transformation elasticity ($\theta$) shifts across distinct historical eras. Mapping the dual-channel mechanism of technical change onto the institutional history of center and peripheral economies yields three core hypotheses regarding the structural evolution of capacity formation:

**Hypothesis 1: The Fordist Distributive-Mechanization Nexus in the Center (1947–1973)** Under the institutional settlement of Atlantic Fordism, rising wage shares acted as the primary catalyst for intensive mechanization. Capitalists responded to sustained distributive pressure by aggressively substituting machinery for labor. This wage-induced choice of technique was structurally accommodated by institutionally coupled capacity planning: extensive plant structures and intensive machinery expanded in tandem with the new technical composition of capital. This regime was structurally stabilized by the mid-1950s—leading to a key econometric break in **1956** characterized by post-Korean War defense budget stabilizers, long-horizon corporate planning, and labor union bureaucratization. These institutions locked in capacity designs but began to restrict capacity utilization flexibility, sustaining productive capacity formation until the late-Fordist profit squeeze (circa 1968–1973) exhausted the regime's mediating capacity, triggering a systemic crisis of accumulation.

**Hypothesis 2: Post-Fordist Institutional Decoupling and Unproductive Overaccumulation in the Center (1974–Present)** Under Post-Fordism, the suppression of labor's bargaining power neutralized the distributive driver of mechanization. This transition was triggered by the structural breaks of **1974** (the Bretton Woods collapse, OPEC oil shock, and severe recession) and **1980** (the Volcker interest rate shock and Reagan's anti-labor counter-offensive). These shocks permanently depressed the baseline level of capacity validation by establishing a neoliberal regime of wage repression and prioritizing short-horizon, financialized asset management over physical capacity utilization. While aggregate capital accumulation continued, it fundamentally decoupled from structural plant expansion. In the absence of wage-induced productive innovation, the _relative price channel_ (the cheapening of short-horizon equipment and ICT) dominated the choice of technique. Capital was increasingly allocated to non-productive, financially reversible processes rather than long-lived productive capacities. This institutional decoupling generated a chronic capacity realization failure, resulting in a severe structural overaccumulation regime where marginal capital growth yields diminishing or negative potential capacity expansion.

**Hypothesis 3: Peripheral Subordination and the Balance of Payments Truncation (1920–Present)** In the capitalist periphery, the transformation of accumulation into productive capacity is structurally truncated by external financial subordination and the Balance of Payments (BoP) constraint. Unlike the center, where domestic class compromise or financialization dictates the choice of technique, peripheral mechanization relies heavily on imported capital goods and technologically non-substitutable inputs. When external financing compressions bind—whether under the inward-looking state-led regimes of the mid-century or the financialized neoliberal export models of the post-Fordist era—foreign exchange scarcity generates a hard realization bottleneck. The effective profit share available for reinvestment is squeezed, blocking the materialization of accounting surplus into productive capacity. This external subordination forces a chronic peripheral accumulation wedge: nominal profitability coexists with truncated mechanization and stalled structural envelopes. Consequently, the peripheral productive ceiling is locked between domestic distributive struggles and the disciplinary regime of global financial markets, permanently destabilizing the long-run coupling between plant and machinery.

These hypotheses establish that the productive ceiling is not a neutral engineering constraint, but a contingent outcome of decentralized accumulation, the balance of class power, and a country's geopolitical position within the world system. The immediate task is to translate these historical-institutional claims into a formal mathematical architecture. Section 3 will therefore formalize the dual-channel choice of technique, the heterogeneous composition of capital, and the peripheral foreign-exchange bottleneck to derive the regime-specific transformation elasticity ($\theta$) that governs the passage from accumulation to productive capacity.
