\subsection{Historical Regimes of Capacity Formation in the
Center and the Periphery}

\paragraph{2.24}
The dual-channel mechanism established in the preceding
subsection operates through historically specific institutional
configurations that shift across accumulation regimes. The
transformation elasticity $\theta$ is not a stable structural
parameter; it is the emergent outcome of class compromise,
geopolitical positionality, and the institutional mediation
of distributive--technological mapping
\citep{Aglietta2000, Vidal2019}. This section grounds those
shifts in the comparative institutional history of center and
peripheral capacity formation, identifying the regime-specific
confinements that bounded structural drift without restoring
proportional reproduction.

\paragraph{2.25}
Under Atlantic Fordism (1947--1973), the center economies
operated under a strong-labor closure in which the wage share
remained approximately constant and real wages grew in tandem
with labor productivity \citep{Vidal2015, Vidal2019}. The
class compromise---epitomized by the Treaty of Detroit (1950)
between General Motors and the United Auto Workers, and by the
Bremen Agreement (1954) in the German metal industry---linked
wages to productivity increases with annual cost-of-living
adjustments. Oligopolistic domestic markets, vertically
integrated firms with internal labor markets, and Keynesian
welfare states institutionalized a virtuous circle of mass
production and mass consumption. Rising real wages offset
underconsumption tendencies; productivity growth from Fordist
methods and intensive accumulation offset the rising organic
composition of capital \citep{Aglietta2000}.

\paragraph{2.26}
The descriptive record confirms this coupling. The US corporate
profit rate averaged $21\%$ from 1947 to 1966, sustained by
a continuous rise in productivity that offset the rising
capital--labor ratio \citep{Vidal2019}. GDP growth in the top
five OECD economies averaged $5.4\%$ per annum between 1950
and 1973, with productivity averaging $4.1\%$
\citep{Maddison1982}. Capital formation remained high: the
US, Germany, and the United Kingdom all sustained gross fixed
capital formation above $20\%$ of GDP throughout the period.
Capacity utilization in US manufacturing averaged above $83\%$
from 1967 to 1979, indicating that accumulation translated
efficiently into realized output. The institutional
configuration $\Lambda$ of Atlantic Fordism constituted a
\textit{confinement}: it restricted structural drift within a
bounded corridor of accumulation, channeling distributive
pressure into wage-led mechanization without triggering
proportional restoration \citep{Vidal2019}.

\paragraph{2.27}
The crisis of Fordism arrived not as a single shock but as
the exhaustion of this confinement. The US profit rate began
a steady decline from 1966, falling from $21\%$ to a trough
of $9\%$ by 1982. The German rate peaked at $18\%$ in 1955
and dropped to $8\%$ by 1981. The UK rate fell continuously
from an inflated postwar high of $44\%$ in 1950 to $6\%$ in
1975 \citep{Vidal2019}. The evidence indicates that the
decline was driven jointly by a rising organic composition
of capital and a profit squeeze from increasing labor shares
\citep{Glyn1990, Wolff2009}. The year 1973 was a watershed:
marked declines in output, productivity, profit rates, and
export growth occurred simultaneously across the OECD,
alongside the collapse of Bretton Woods and the first OPEC
oil shock. The institutional mediation that had contained
structural drift could no longer hold. The Fordist
confinement was exhausted.

\paragraph{2.28}
The post-Fordist restructuring that followed suppressed the
distributive driver of mechanization and decoupled accumulation
from structural capacity formation. The Volcker interest rate
shock (1979), the Reagan--Thatcher anti-labor offensive, and
the collapse of union density dismantled the strong-labor
closure. In the United States, the wage share peaked at $67\%$
in 1970 and fell to $64\%$ by 1997, while nonwage
compensation---mainly health care---rose faster than wages,
meaning actual employee income dropped even more sharply, from
$60\%$ in 1970 to $51\%$ in 2007 \citep{Kliman2012,
Vidal2019}. Union density fell in all three countries, with
simple correlations between union density and labor's share of
$0.65$ (US), $0.61$ (UK), and $0.62$ (Germany) for the period
1960--2010 \citep{Vidal2019}.

\paragraph{2.29}
Under the weak-labor closure that replaced Fordism, the
relative price channel---the cheapening of short-horizon
equipment and ICT---dominated the choice of technique, while
the distributive channel atrophied. Capital was increasingly
allocated to financially reversible assets rather than
long-lived productive capacities. Total capacity utilization
in US manufacturing fell from an average of $83.2\%$
(1967--1979) to $80.8\%$ in the 1980s, $82.4\%$ in the
1990s, and $77.7\%$ in the 2000s, demonstrating increasing
overcapacity \citep{Vidal2019}. GDP growth decelerated
secularly: the United States managed $3.2\%$ per annum over
the 1970s--1990s but only $1.7\%$ in the 2000s; Germany fell
from $3.07\%$ in the 1970s to $1.17\%$ in the 2000s
\citep{Vidal2019}. Household debt-to-income ratios rose by
$61\%$ in the US and $60\%$ in the UK between 1995 and 2008,
indicating that moderate growth was sustained by debt-led
consumption rather than productive investment
\citep{Stockhammer2012}. The post-Fordist configuration
represents the \textit{erosion of confinement}: the
institutional mediation that previously bounded structural
drift has been exhausted, and the regime can no longer contain
the disproportionality it generates. Marginal capital growth
yields diminishing or negative capacity expansion: the
aggregate transformation elasticity falls below unity,
producing chronic industrial stagnation
\citep{Vidal2013, Vidal2019}.

\paragraph{2.30}
In the capitalist periphery, the transformation of accumulation
into productive capacity is structurally truncated by external
financial subordination and the balance of payments constraint
\citep{Prebisch1981, Vernengo2006}. Unlike the center, where
domestic class compromise or financialization dictates the
choice of technique, peripheral mechanization relies heavily on
imported capital goods and technologically non-substitutable
inputs. The BoP constraint operates as a hard realization
bottleneck, but its institutional mechanism shifts across
distinct historical phases of external subordination.

\paragraph{2.31}
Under the developmentalist coupling (1933--1973), the BoP
constraint operated primarily as an \textit{import capacity
ceiling}: foreign exchange scarcity limited the importation of
capital goods, imposing a hard ceiling on the mechanization gap
$\kappa_t$. Peripheral adaptation to Atlantic Fordism took the
form of import-substituting industrialization, in which the
state actively promoted domestic manufacturing behind tariff
walls while the economy remained tethered to the world market
through the demand for imported machinery
\citep{Cardoso1972, Silva2007}. The institutional
configuration was shaped by the oligarchic-industrial fusion:
in Chile, the landowning elite and the nascent industrial
bourgeoisie were integrated through banking and the nitrate
cycle, producing a class structure in which the capitalist
class lacked autonomy from agrarian property
\citep{Zeitlin2014, Richards1979}.

\paragraph{2.32}
A brief descriptive illustration grounds the peripheral
hypothesis. In Chile, the inward-looking industrialization
phase sustained gross capital formation at approximately $5\%$
per year, yet manufacturing never exceeded $19\%$ of GDP, and
the services sector surpassed $44\%$ by 1970
\citep{DeVylder1976}. The \textit{campo-ciudad} transition
regulated the distributive conflict: urban population growth
averaged $5\%$ annually in the 1950s, sustaining a Lewis-type
labor surplus that disciplined urban wages
\citep{Lewis1954}. Once this demographic transition
exhausted---urban growth decelerated to $3\%$ in the 1960s,
and the labor force growth rate fell from approximately $3\%$
to below $1\%$---the working class improved its bargaining
position decisively \citep{Shaikh2016}. The wage share jumped
from $42.7\%$ in 1970 to $50.6\%$ in 1971 under the Unidad
Popular, while union affiliation rose from approximately $10\%$
to $25\%$ and average union size grew from 80 to 120 workers.
The distributive breakthrough coincided with the collapse of
the import capacity ceiling: the effective profit share
available for productive reinvestment was squeezed between
rising wages and the structural inelasticity of agricultural
supply, generating chronic inflation and a BoP crisis that
culminated in the regime's violent termination
\citep{DeVylder1976, Sunkel1958}.

\paragraph{2.33}
Under neoliberal subordination (1975--present), the BoP
constraint morphed from an import capacity ceiling into a
\textit{policy space ceiling}. The neoliberal refoundation
dismantled the developmentalist architecture through radical
trade liberalization---Chilean tariffs fell from $94\%$ in
1973 to a uniform $10\%$ by 1979---financial deregulation,
and the elimination of capital controls
\citep{Ahumada2019, FfrenchDavis2015}. Manufacturing fell from
$30\%$ of GDP in 1974 to $20.4\%$ by 1982, as cheap imports
and exchange rate appreciation destroyed import-substituting
industry \citep{Ahumada2019}. The subsequent democratic period
(1990--1997) produced a growth boom averaging $6\%$ per year,
but this boom rested on natural resource exports and FDI
inflows rather than endogenous industrial upgrading.

\paragraph{2.34}
The consolidation of passive integration through the WTO
(1995) and a wave of bilateral free trade agreements---with
the United States (2004), the European Union (2004), and
China (2006)---legally encoded the policy space restriction.
Capital controls, export subsidies, FDI performance
requirements, and flexible intellectual property regimes were
eliminated or severely curtailed \citep{Shadlen2005,
Gallagher2008}. The commodity boom (2004--2012) temporarily
relaxed the BoP constraint through Chinese demand for copper
and natural resources, but reinforced extractive
specialization and premature deindustrialization. By 2016,
natural resources and resource-based manufactures accounted
for $90\%$ of Chilean exports; manufacturing had fallen to
$12\%$ of GDP; and FDI concentrated overwhelmingly in mining
($33\%$ of total inflows, 2009--2016) and financial services,
with negligible technological spillover to national firms
\citep{Ahumada2019, ECLAC2013}. The peripheral productive
ceiling is locked between domestic distributive struggle and
the disciplinary regime of global financial markets,
permanently destabilizing the long-run coupling between plant
and machinery.

\paragraph{2.35}
These historical configurations establish that the productive
ceiling is not a neutral engineering constraint but a
contingent outcome of decentralized accumulation, the balance
of class power, and a country's geopolitical position within
the world system. The center--periphery comparison reveals a
structural asymmetry: in the center, the exhaustion of
confinement produces crisis and restructuring; in the
periphery, the absence of confinement produces permanent
truncation. The immediate task is to translate these
historical-institutional claims into a formal mathematical
architecture. Section 3 will therefore formalize the
dual-channel choice of technique, the heterogeneous
composition of capital, and the peripheral foreign-exchange
bottleneck to derive the regime-specific transformation
elasticity ($\theta$) that governs the passage from
accumulation to productive capacity. It closes with a
consolidated empirical identification strategy that translates
these formal results into estimable cointegrating
specifications.

# Hypothesis materials 




\subsection{Historical Regimes of Capacity Formation in the Center and the Periphery}

\paragraph{A}
The theoretical framework developed above—combining Okishio’s market anarchy, Vidal’s divided firm, and the Marx-Okishio threshold—provides the political economy foundation for understanding how the transformation elasticity ($\theta$) shifts across distinct historical eras. Mapping the dual-channel mechanism of technical change onto the institutional history of center and peripheral economies yields three core hypotheses regarding the structural evolution of capacity formation:

\paragraph{B}
\textbf{Hypothesis 1: The Fordist Distributive-Mechanization Nexus in the Center (1947–1973)} Under the institutional settlement of Atlantic Fordism, rising wage shares acted as the primary catalyst for intensive mechanization. Capitalists responded to sustained distributive pressure by aggressively substituting machinery for labor. This wage-induced choice of technique was structurally accommodated by institutionally coupled capacity planning: extensive plant structures and intensive machinery expanded in tandem with the new technical composition of capital. This regime was structurally stabilized by the mid-1950s—leading to a key econometric break in \textbf{1956} characterized by post-Korean War defense budget stabilizers, long-horizon corporate planning, and labor union bureaucratization. These institutions locked in capacity designs but began to restrict capacity utilization flexibility, sustaining productive capacity formation until the late-Fordist profit squeeze (circa 1968–1973) exhausted the regime's mediating capacity, triggering a systemic crisis of accumulation.

\paragraph{C}
\textbf{Hypothesis 2: Post-Fordist Institutional Decoupling and Unproductive Overaccumulation in the Center (1974–Present)} Under Post-Fordism, the suppression of labor's bargaining power neutralized the distributive driver of mechanization. This transition was triggered by the structural breaks of \textbf{1974} (the Bretton Woods collapse, OPEC oil shock, and severe recession) and \textbf{1980} (the Volcker interest rate shock and Reagan's anti-labor counter-offensive). These shocks permanently depressed the baseline level of capacity validation by establishing a neoliberal regime of wage repression and prioritizing short-horizon, financialized asset management over physical capacity utilization. While aggregate capital accumulation continued, it fundamentally decoupled from structural plant expansion. In the absence of wage-induced productive innovation, the \textit{relative price channel} (the cheapening of short-horizon equipment and ICT) dominated the choice of technique. Capital was increasingly allocated to non-productive, financially reversible processes rather than long-lived productive capacities. This institutional decoupling generated a chronic capacity realization failure, resulting in a severe structural overaccumulation regime where marginal capital growth yields diminishing or negative potential capacity expansion.

\paragraph{D}
\textbf{Hypothesis 3: Peripheral Subordination and the Balance of Payments Truncation (1920–Present)} In the capitalist periphery, the transformation of accumulation into productive capacity is structurally truncated by external financial subordination and the Balance of Payments (BoP) constraint. Unlike the center, where domestic class compromise or financialization dictates the choice of technique, peripheral mechanization relies heavily on imported capital goods and technologically non-substitutable inputs. When external financing compressions bind—whether under the inward-looking state-led regimes of the mid-century or the financialized neoliberal export models of the post-Fordist era—foreign exchange scarcity generates a hard realization bottleneck. The effective profit share available for reinvestment is squeezed, blocking the materialization of accounting surplus into productive capacity. This external subordination forces a chronic peripheral accumulation wedge: nominal profitability coexists with truncated mechanization and stalled structural envelopes. Consequently, the peripheral productive ceiling is locked between domestic distributive struggles and the disciplinary regime of global financial markets, permanently destabilizing the long-run coupling between plant and machinery.

\paragraph{E}
These hypotheses establish that the productive ceiling is not a neutral engineering constraint, but a contingent outcome of decentralized accumulation, the balance of class power, and a country's geopolitical position within the world system. The immediate task is to translate these historical-institutional claims into a formal mathematical architecture. Section 3 will therefore formalize the dual-channel choice of technique, the heterogeneous composition of capital, and the peripheral foreign-exchange bottleneck to derive the regime-specific transformation elasticity ($\theta$) that governs the passage from accumulation to productive capacity.
