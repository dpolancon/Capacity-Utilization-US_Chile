# Beyond the I(2) Trap: A Methodological Guide to Estimating Time-Varying Elasticities in Interactive Cointegrating Regressions

This report provides a comprehensive methodological analysis for estimating a non-linear interactive time series model using super-consistent cointegration estimators. The central model under investigation is specified as $y_t = a + b x_t + c y_{t-1} + d (x_t \cdot y_{t-1}) + e_t$. The primary objective is to consistently estimate the coefficient `d`, which captures a state-dependent elasticity, by adapting established single-equation cointegration techniques—Fully Modified OLS (FM-OLS), Dynamic OLS (DOLS), and Integrated Modified OLS (IM-OLS)—to this specific interactive framework. The analysis addresses the profound theoretical challenges posed by the integration properties of the interaction term, the practical issues of multicollinearity, and the nuanced identification of the elasticity parameter. Grounded in foundational and modern econometric literature, this report offers a rigorous roadmap for researchers seeking to apply these methods with precision and statistical validity.

## Navigating the "I(2) Trap" and the Role of Polynomial Cointegration

A fundamental and often overlooked challenge in estimating interactive cointegrating regressions lies in the integration order of the interaction term. The model $y_t = a + b x_t + c y_{t-1} + d (x_t \cdot y_{t-1}) + e_t$ contains the product term $(x_t \cdot y_{t-1})$. Assuming the underlying variables $x_t$ and $y_t$ are integrated of order one, denoted $I(1)$, their product is generally integrated of order two, $I(2)$ [[33](https://grokipedia.com/page/Cointegration), [136](https://moodle2.units.it/pluginfile.php/259621/mod_resource/content/0/Chap8.pdf)]. This phenomenon, colloquially termed the "I(2) trap," presents a significant theoretical obstacle because standard super-consistent estimators like FM-OLS, DOLS, and IM-OLS were originally developed for linear regressions where all regressors share the same order of integration, typically $I(1)$ [[156](https://www.econstor.eu/bitstream/10419/68500/1/734422709.pdf), [312](https://www.jstor.org/stable/2171721)]. Applying these estimators directly to a model containing an $I(2)$ regressor without a suitable theoretical framework is statistically invalid and risks producing misleading inference [[198](http://gnteam.cs.manchester.ac.uk/old/epidemiology/data/specialist.txt)].

The resolution to this problem requires moving beyond conventional linear cointegration theory and embracing more advanced frameworks designed to handle mixed-order integrated variables. The most relevant approach is **Polynomial Cointegration**, which explicitly allows for regression models that include powers and products of integrated processes as explanatory variables [[138](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/282627505_COINTEGRATING_POLYNOMIAL_REGRESSIONS_FULLY_MODIFIED_OLS_ESTIMATION_AND_INFERENCE), [148](https://www.mendeley.com/catalogue/1bcfafc2-e0f0-3f0c-a7d6-50100f4bb927/)]. In this context, the model becomes a Cointegrating Polynomial Regression (CPR). The core question shifts from whether two $I(1)$ series are cointegrated to whether a linear combination of variables of different integration orders ($I(1)$ and $I(2)$) can result in a stationary residual process, $e_t$. For the specified model to represent a valid CPR, the stochastic trends inherent in the $I(1)$ variables $x_t$ and $y_{t-1}$, and the stronger stochastic trend in the $I(2)$ interaction term, must cancel out perfectly in the long run through the parameters `a`, `b`, `c`, and `d`. This imposes strict conditions on the parameters, ensuring that the deterministic and stochastic components balance to yield a mean-reverting error term [[18](https://irihs.ihs.ac.at/id/eprint/2047/1/es-264.pdf), [22](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/COINTEGRATING-POLYNOMIAL-REGRESSIONS%3A-FULLY-OLS-AND-Wagner-Hong/c75d95ff0ef702649af0e61006a779a5804c3098)].

Fortunately, modern econometric research has extended the application of FM-OLS, DOLS, and IM-OLS to the polynomial cointegration setting. These extensions modify the original estimators' formulas to correctly account for the distinct asymptotic properties of the mixed-order regressors [[141](https://www.bsi.si/storage/uploads/7b765046-0e5c-4dba-bdcc-1b313cf599fa/Integrated-Modified-Least-.pdf), [145](https://www.econstor.eu/bitstream/10419/68510/1/734405871.pdf), [337](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Panel-cointegrating-polynomial-regression-analysis-Jong-Wagner/678424d7d24016798c81a90c7136c7a983b080a5), [338](https://core.ac.uk/download/pdf/189452423.pdf)]. For instance, the fully modified OLS estimator has been generalized to CPRs, demonstrating robustness and maintaining its desirable limiting distribution properties even with the inclusion of powered and product terms [[223](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/5600F32C6B793E8C618348FA3523E59B/S0266466624000033a.pdf/cointegrating_polynomial_regressions_robustness_of_fully_modified_ols.pdf), [224](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/econometric-theory/article/cointegrating-polynomial-regressions-robustness-of-fully-modified-ols/5600F32C6B793E8C618348FA3523E59B)]. Similarly, IM-OLS, introduced by Vogelsang and Wagner (2014), has also been adapted for multivariate polynomial regressions, allowing for arbitrary cross-products of powers of integrated variables [[172](https://www.mdpi.com/2225-1146/9/1/12), [238](https://www.ssoar.info/ssoar/handle/document/94360?locale-attribute=en), [313](https://d-nb.info/1138836575/34)]. This body of work confirms that while the "I(2) trap" invalidates naive applications, it does not render the estimation problem insoluble. Instead, it mandates the adoption of the more general CPR framework, for which robust estimation techniques have been developed.

While Polynomial Cointegration is the most direct and applicable framework, another avenue is full-fledged **I(2) cointegration analysis**. This approach is typically employed within a multi-equation system, such as a Vector Autoregressive (VAR) model, and was pioneered by researchers like Søren Johansen [[1](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0304407697000882)]. An I(2) system implies that some linear combinations of the variables are $I(1)$, while others are $I(0)$, requiring a more complex decomposition of the VAR representation [[134](https://www.researchgate.net/post/How_to_check_cointegration_of_variables_that_are_both_I2)]. However, since the research goal specifies a single-equation interactive model, adopting a full I(2) VAR framework would be overly complex and potentially misspecified if no other equations in the system are available. Therefore, the Polynomial Cointegration approach is the most pragmatic and theoretically sound path forward. It directly addresses the issue within the single-equation context, providing a coherent framework to define and estimate the long-run relationship despite the presence of the $I(2)$ interaction term.

In summary, the "I(2) trap" is a critical theoretical hurdle that cannot be ignored. Its existence necessitates a departure from standard linear cointegration theory. The correct approach involves recognizing the model as a Cointegrating Polynomial Regression (CPR) and utilizing the specialized adaptations of FM-OLS, DOLS, and IM-OLS designed for this class of models. This ensures that the estimation procedure properly accounts for the different integration orders of the regressors, thereby achieving consistent and asymptotically valid estimation of the parameters, including the crucial coefficient `d`.

## Adaptation of FM-OLS, DOLS, and IM-OLS to Non-Linear Models

Once the theoretical foundation of Polynomial Cointegration (CPR) is accepted, the next step is to understand how the three key super-consistent estimators—FM-OLS, DOLS, and IM-OLS—are mathematically adapted to accommodate the non-linear interaction term $(x_t \cdot y_{t-1})$. Each estimator modifies the basic Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) regression in a distinct way to correct for the biases induced by endogeneity and serial correlation in non-stationary data.

**Fully Modified OLS (FM-OLS)**, introduced by Phillips and Hansen (1990), operates by adding bias-correction terms to the standard OLS estimates [[7](https://econweb.ucsd.edu/~yisun/homogenous_coint_paper.pdf), [15](https://www.jstor.org/stable/43948013)]. These corrections are derived from the long-run covariance (LRCV) matrix of the structural errors. In the context of a CPR, the adaptation is twofold. First, the LRCV calculation must be expanded to include the interaction term. The covariance matrix will now contain elements representing the covariances between the innovations of the $I(1)$ variables $x_t$ and $y_{t-1}$, the innovation of the $I(2)$ interaction term, and their cross-covariances [[21](https://ideas.repec.org/a/cup/etheor/v32y2016i05p1289-1315_00.html)]. Second, the correction terms themselves are constructed based on this augmented LRCV matrix. The FM-OLS estimator thus adjusts the initial OLS coefficient estimates for the endogeneity of the regressors and the serial correlation in the error term by accounting for the entire spectral density at frequency zero of the vector of regressors and errors [[156](https://www.econstor.eu/bitstream/10419/68500/1/734422709.pdf)]. The resulting FM-OLS estimator for CPRs has been shown to possess a zero-mean Gaussian mixture limiting distribution, ensuring the validity of standard asymptotic inference procedures [[145](https://www.econstor.eu/bitstream/10419/68510/1/734405871.pdf), [338](https://core.ac.uk/download/pdf/189452423.pdf)]. This makes FM-OLS a powerful tool, though its finite-sample performance can be sensitive to the choice of kernel function and bandwidth for LRCV estimation [[75](https://www.wouterdenhaan.com/papers/HAND30.pdf), [77](https://www.ssc.wisc.edu/~bhansen/718/NeweyWest1994.pdf)].

**Dynamic OLS (DOLS)**, proposed by Saikkonen (1991) and Stock and Watson (1993), takes a different approach [[120](http://www.columbia.edu/~sn2294/pub/joe97.pdf), [389](https://eldorado.tu-dortmund.de/server/api/core/bitstreams/c42dc150-b798-4eaa-a229-4f113b6cf50c/content)]. Instead of modifying the OLS estimator with correction terms, DOLS augments the regression equation with leads and lags of the first-differenced regressors to purge the regressors of their stochastic trend component and make them asymptotically orthogonal to the error term [[51](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S235248472400461X), [299](https://psecommunity.org/wp-content/plugins/wpor/includes/file/2303/LAPSE-2023.25463-1v1.pdf)]. When applied to the interactive model, this augmentation must be carefully specified. For each regressor—including the interaction term—one must include a set of leads and lags of its first difference. Consequently, the DOLS regression for the interactive model would include leads and lags of $\Delta x_t$, leads and lags of $\Delta y_{t-1}$, and, critically, leads and lags of the first difference of the interaction term, $\Delta(x_t \cdot y_{t-1})$ [[54](https://publication.aercafricalibrary.org/bitstream/123456789/1648/1/CMAP%20ECON%20561%20Econometrics%20Theory%20and%20Practice%20I%20-%20Lecture%20Notes%202020.pdf), [217](https://www.icoaef.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/ICOAEFXIV_FULL_PAPER_25122025.pdf)]. While this approach elegantly solves the endogeneity and serial correlation problems, it introduces a new practical challenge: a rapidly growing number of regressors. Including multiple leads and lags for the interaction term can severely inflate the number of parameters to be estimated, leading to a loss of degrees of freedom and potential small-sample bias [[93](https://www.ifo.de/DocDL/cesifo1_wp1565.pdf)]. The choice of the optimal number of leads and lags is therefore a critical empirical decision, often guided by information criteria or fixed conventions (e.g., one lag and one lead) [[54](https://publication.aercafricalibrary.org/bitstream/123456789/1648/1/CMAP%20ECON%20561%20Econometrics%20Theory%20and%20Practice%20I%20-%20Lecture%20Notes%202020.pdf), [310](https://irihs.ihs.ac.at/id/eprint/2037/1/es-263.pdf)].

**Integrated Modified OLS (IM-OLS)**, developed by Vogelsang and Wagner (2014), offers a third, conceptually distinct approach that sidesteps some of the pitfalls of the other two estimators [[4](https://www.econstor.eu/bitstream/10419/290890/1/1887039155.pdf), [140](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/econom/v178y2014i2p741-760.html)]. IM-OLS is algebraically similar to FM-OLS but is derived from a partial sum (integration) transformation of the regression model rather than a modification of the OLS criterion function [[16](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304407613002303), [139](https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/cointReg/cointReg.pdf)]. Its key advantage is that it avoids the explicit estimation of long-run variance matrices, which can be difficult and imprecise in practice, especially with short time series [[155](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/254423186_Integrated_Modified_OLS_Estimation_and_Fixed-b_Inference_for_Cointegrating_Regressions)]. Like FM-OLS and DOLS, IM-OLS has been successfully extended to the CPR framework [[172](https://www.mdpi.com/2225-1146/9/1/12), [313](https://d-nb.info/1138836575/34)]. The integration transformation is applied to each regressor, including the interaction term, which implicitly handles the serial correlation and endogeneity issues without needing separate correction terms or an explosion of leads and lags. This makes IM-OLS particularly appealing for models with complex regressor sets, as it maintains computational simplicity while delivering consistent and robust estimates. Studies show that the IM-OLS residual-based cointegration test performs similarly to the FM-OLS test and possesses good power properties against various alternatives [[239](https://eldorado.tu-dortmund.de/bitstream/2003/36054/1/Dissertation_Grabarczyk.pdf), [314](https://eldorado.tu-dortmund.de/server/api/core/bitstreams/515bbc99-19b3-429a-bb93-55f89f729970/content)].

The following table summarizes the adaptation of these estimators for the interactive cointegrating regression model.

| Estimator | Core Mechanism for Interactive Model | Key Adaptation Requirement | Potential Pitfall |
|---|---|---|---|
| **FM-OLS** | Adds bias-correction terms to OLS estimates based on the Long-Run Covariance (LRCV) matrix [[156](https://www.econstor.eu/bitstream/10419/68500/1/734422709.pdf)]. | The LRCV matrix must be calculated for the full set of regressors, including the $I(2)$ interaction term $(x_t \cdot y_{t-1})$. | Finite-sample performance depends on the choice of kernel and bandwidth for LRCV estimation [[77](https://www.ssc.wisc.edu/~bhansen/718/NeweyWest1994.pdf)]. |
| **DOLS** | Augments the regression with leads and lags of the first differences of the regressors [[51](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S235248472400461X)]. | Must include leads and lags of $\Delta x_t$, $\Delta y_{t-1}$, and $\Delta(x_t \cdot y_{t-1})$. | Can lead to a large number of regressors, causing a loss of degrees of freedom and small-sample bias [[93](https://www.ifo.de/DocDL/cesifo1_wp1565.pdf)]. |
| **IM-OLS** | Applies an integration transformation to the regression model, avoiding explicit LRCV estimation [[16](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304407613002303)]. | The integration transformation is applied to each regressor, including the interaction term [[313](https://d-nb.info/1138836575/34)]. | Less direct in its correction compared to FM-OLS, though empirically robust [[170](https://ideas.repec.org/p/ihs/ihswps/53.html)]. |

In conclusion, all three estimators can be formally adapted for use in the interactive cointegrating regression model within the Polynomial Cointegration framework. FM-OLS requires careful estimation of a complex LRCV matrix. DOLS requires the inclusion of leads and lags for the interaction term, risking a sparse design matrix. IM-OLS presents a compelling alternative by avoiding both LRV estimation and the cumbersome leads/lags of the interaction term, making it a theoretically elegant and computationally simpler choice for this specific non-linear application.

## Managing Severe Multicollinearity in Interactive Specifications

The inclusion of an interaction term $(x_t \cdot y_{t-1})$ alongside its constituent variables $x_t$ and $y_{t-1}$, and further compounded by the addition of leads, lags, and differences required by DOLS and FM-OLS, creates a high-risk environment for severe multicollinearity [[100](https://medium.com/@WriteWithAK/the-diagnostic-triad-autocorrelation-heteroskedasticity-multicollinearity-unveiled-723209a59502), [271](https://zalamsyah.staff.unja.ac.id/wp-content/uploads/sites/286/2019/11/7-Basic-Econometrics-4th-Ed.-Gujarati.pdf)]. Multicollinearity arises when there is a high degree of linear intercorrelation among the explanatory variables in a regression model [[381](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/334468147_Multicollinearity_and_misleading_statistical_results)]. In this case, the interaction term is inherently correlated with $x_t$ and $y_{t-1}$, and this correlation is exacerbated when lagged and differenced versions of these variables are added to the regressor matrix, creating a near-singular design matrix [[67](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4888898/), [328](https://www.statalist.org/forums/forum/general-stata-discussion/general/1606489-multicollinearity-in-lagged-independent-variables), [329](https://www.reddit.com/r/econometrics/comments/obl7wf/multicollinearity_in_distributed_lag_models/)]. While some argue that multicollinearity is an "over-rated statistical issue" in certain contexts [[63](https://www.statalist.org/forums/forum/general-stata-discussion/general/1359532-is-multicollinearity-between-interaction-terms-a-problem)], its adverse impact on the precision of coefficient estimates, the instability of the model, and the unreliability of statistical inference in finite samples is well-documented [[104](https://joiv.org/index.php/joiv/article/viewFile/3012/1383), [334](https://ww2.amstat.org/meetings/proceedings/2017/data/assets/pdf/594012.pdf)]. Therefore, a proactive strategy for diagnosing and mitigating multicollinearity is essential for obtaining reliable estimates of the elasticity parameter `d`.

The first step in any multicollinearity management strategy is rigorous diagnosis. The most common and effective tools for this purpose are the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) and condition indices.
*   **Variance Inflation Factor (VIF):** The VIF measures how much the variance of an estimated regression coefficient is increased due to multicollinearity. A VIF value greater than 10 is often cited as a strong indicator of severe multicollinearity, suggesting that the variable is highly correlated with other regressors in the model [[62](https://www.researchgate.net/post/Which_interaction_terms_should_I_include_for_my_regression_analysis), [89](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Variance-Inflation-Factor-and-Condition-Number-in-Salmer%C3%B3n-Garc%C3%ADa/b76d7a30919d1a4165896fb36cbb6146a1b2eddd)]. R packages like `mctest` provide convenient functions to compute VIFs for all regressors [[85](https://journal.r-project.org/articles/RJ-2016-062/)].
*   **Condition Indices and Variance Decomposition Proportions:** These metrics offer a more detailed analysis of the sources of multicollinearity. The condition number of the scaled regressor matrix is calculated; a condition number above 30 suggests the presence of numerical instability due to multicollinearity [[82](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6900425/), [84](https://www.scribd.com/document/795958444/condition-number)]. High variance decomposition proportions for a given condition index indicate which variables are involved in the collinear relationships.

Once diagnosed, several mitigation strategies can be employed. These can be broadly categorized into traditional econometric techniques and modern regularization methods.
1.  **Orthogonalization:** This is a powerful technique rooted in the Frisch-Waugh-Lovell theorem. The idea is to transform the regressors so they are mutually orthogonal. For the interaction term, this can be achieved by regressing $(x_t \cdot y_{t-1})$ on the main effects $x_t$ and $y_{t-1}$, and then using the residuals from this auxiliary regression as the new instrument for the interaction effect [[285](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/258136259_Orthogonalizing_Through_Residual_Centering), [288](https://www.statalist.org/forums/forum/general-stata-discussion/general/501263-stata-tip-118-orthogonalizing-powered-and-product-terms-using-residual-centering)]. This process effectively removes the linear dependence of the interaction term on the individual variables while preserving its unique non-linear information. Orthogonalizing powered and product terms using residual centering is a recommended practice in regression analysis [[288](https://www.statalist.org/forums/forum/general-stata-discussion/general/501263-stata-tip-118-orthogonalizing-powered-and-product-terms-using-residual-centering), [333](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/243043572_On_the_Merits_of_Orthogonalizing_Powered_and_Product_Terms_Implications_for_Modeling_Interactions_Among_Latent_Variables)].
2.  **Mean Centering:** A simpler approach is to mean-center the variables $x_t$ and $y_{t-1}$ before creating the interaction term. This reduces, but does not eliminate, the correlation between the interaction term and its main effects, and it simplifies the interpretation of the coefficients [[64](https://stats.stackexchange.com/questions/606803/multicollinearity-and-interaction-effects), [282](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6713984/)]. However, it does not solve the deeper problem of multicollinearity that persists with lagged and differenced variables.
3.  **Regularization (Ridge Regression):** This machine learning-inspired technique offers a robust solution to multicollinearity. Ridge regression introduces a penalty term (the L2 norm of the coefficients) to the OLS objective function [[421](https://www.ibm.com/think/topics/ridge-regression)]. This constrains the size of the coefficients, shrinking them towards zero. By doing so, it dramatically reduces the variance of the coefficient estimates at the cost of introducing a small amount of bias [[417](https://journal.pandawainstitute.com/index.php/jmans/article/view/251)]. This trade-off results in more stable and reliable predictions, especially when the design matrix is ill-conditioned [[47](https://www.mdpi.com/2227-7390/10/8/1283)]. The connection between ridge regression and smoothing splines, and its representation of time-varying parameters, highlights its relevance for dynamic econometric models [[360](https://arxiv.org/html/2009.00401v4), [416](https://stats.stackexchange.com/questions/81395/relationship-between-ridge-regression-and-pca-regression)]. Given that the super-consistency of OLS is an asymptotic property, regularization provides a valuable tool for improving finite-sample performance in the face of severe multicollinearity.

In conclusion, the interactive nature of the model specification makes it exceptionally vulnerable to multicollinearity. Ignoring this issue can lead to unreliable estimates of the elasticity parameter `d` and invalid statistical tests. A systematic approach involving thorough diagnostic checks followed by an appropriate mitigation strategy—preferably orthogonalization or ridge regression—is not merely an optional refinement but a necessary step for credible empirical analysis.

## Empirical Strategy and Diagnostic Procedures

A successful empirical application of super-consistent estimators to an interactive cointegrating regression requires a structured, multi-stage strategy encompassing pre-estimation testing, careful implementation of the chosen estimator, and rigorous post-estimation diagnostics. This software-agnostic roadmap ensures that the analysis is conducted with methodological rigor and that the results are statistically sound.

**Step 1: Pre-Estimation Testing**

Before estimating the interactive model, it is crucial to establish the integration properties of the underlying variables and test for the existence of a long-run relationship.

1.  **Unit Root Testing:** The assumption that $x_t$ and $y_t$ are $I(1)$ must be empirically verified. Conduct robust unit root tests, such as the Augmented Dickey-Fuller (ADF) test, the Phillips-Perron (PP) test, or the KPSS test, on the level series $x_t$ and $y_t$ [[86](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Bq9vexQEk84), [186](https://www.econstor.eu/bitstream/10419/67744/1/616664753.pdf)]. It is advisable to use tests that allow for potential structural breaks, as ignoring breaks can lead to spurious rejections of the unit root null hypothesis [[31](https://www.econstor.eu/bitstream/10419/84837/1/ddpie_111.pdf)]. The goal is to confirm that both variables are non-stationary in their levels but are assumed to be stationary in their first differences.

2.  **Non-Linear Cointegration Testing:** With the variables confirmed to be $I(1)$, the next step is to test for the presence of a long-run equilibrium relationship described by the interactive model. This involves a residual-based approach, analogous to the Engle-Granger two-step method [[184](https://warwick.ac.uk/fac/soc/economics/staff/gboero/personal/hand2_cointeg.pdf), [364](https://metricgate.com/docs/engle-granger-cointegration/)]. First, estimate the cointegrating regression using a consistent method, such as simple OLS on the levels of the variables (which remains consistent in this context) [[221](https://www.uh.edu/~bsorense/coint2022.pdf)]. Second, obtain the residuals from this regression, $\hat{u}_t = y_t - \hat{a} - \hat{b}x_t - \hat{c}y_{t-1} - \hat{d}(x_t \cdot y_{t-1})$. Finally, apply a unit root test (e.g., ADF or PP) to the residual series $\{\hat{u}_t\}$. The null hypothesis of the test is that the residuals are non-stationary ($I(1)$), implying no cointegration. A rejection of this null hypothesis indicates that the residuals are stationary ($I(0)$), providing evidence for a long-run equilibrium relationship, or cointegration, as specified by the interactive model [[125](https://www.bauer.uh.edu/rsusmel/phd/ec2-7.pdf), [279](https://www.facebook.com/groups/hossainacademy/posts/2480891575284125/)]. Specialized residual-based tests for non-linear cointegration should be preferred over linear ones if they are available [[339](https://d-nb.info/1280725737/34), [370](http://www.econ.ryukoku.ac.jp/er_activities/Maki%20and%20Kitasaka(2009).pdf)].

**Step 2: Estimation of the Interactive Model**

Once cointegration is established, proceed with the estimation using one of the adapted super-consistent estimators.

1.  **Model Specification:** Clearly specify the interactive model: $y_t = a + b x_t + c y_{t-1} + d (x_t \cdot y_{t-1}) + e_t$. Create the interaction term as a new regressor.

2.  **Estimator Implementation:**
    *   **For FM-OLS:** Estimate the initial OLS regression. Then, calculate the long-run covariance (LRCV) matrix for the vector of regressors (including the interaction term) using a Heteroskedasticity and Autocorrelation Consistent (HAC) estimator. Use a quadratic spectral kernel with an automatic bandwidth selection method, such as Andrews' method, to ensure robust standard errors [[76](https://sandwich.r-forge.r-project.org/articles/pdf/sandwich.pdf), [260](https://arxiv.org/abs/1604.02724), [377](https://issaasphil.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/06/J.-ISSAAS-Vol-31-no.-1-June-2025-Full-Journal.pdf)]. Apply the resulting bias-correction terms to the initial OLS estimates to obtain the final FM-OLS estimates.
    *   **For DOLS:** Augment the regression with leads and lags of the first-differenced regressors. This includes leads and lags of $\Delta x_t$, $\Delta y_{t-1}$, and $\Delta(x_t \cdot y_{t-1})$. The number of leads and lags (`k`) must be selected. A common practice is to use a fixed number, such as one lag and one lead, or to select `k` based on an information criterion like the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) or Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) [[54](https://publication.aercafricalibrary.org/bitstream/123456789/1648/1/CMAP%20ECON%20561%20Econometrics%20Theory%20and%20Practice%20I%20-%20Lecture%20Notes%202020.pdf), [310](https://irihs.ihs.ac.at/id/eprint/2037/1/es-263.pdf)]. Estimate the augmented regression using OLS.
    *   **For IM-OLS:** Implement the estimation procedure as outlined by Vogelsang and Wagner (2014). This involves transforming the dependent variable and all regressors through a partial sum (integration) operator. The regression is then estimated on these transformed variables, which implicitly corrects for serial correlation and endogeneity without requiring LRCV estimation or lead-lag augmentation [[4](https://www.econstor.eu/bitstream/10419/290890/1/1887039155.pdf), [313](https://d-nb.info/1138836575/34)].

**Step 3: Post-Estimation Diagnostics**

After obtaining the final estimates, a series of diagnostic checks should be performed to validate the model and the estimation procedure.

1.  **Serial Correlation Test:** Although FM-OLS and DOLS are designed to correct for serial correlation, it is prudent to verify that the residuals from the *final modified regression* are free of remaining autocorrelation. Apply a portmanteau test, such as the Ljung-Box test, to the squared residuals of the final model to check for any remaining serial structure [[49](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/373530484_Sensitivity_of_the_Ramsey's_Regression_Specification_Error_Term_Test_on_the_Degree_of_Nonlinearity_of_the_Functional_Form)].

2.  **Endogeneity Assessment:** While the estimators are designed to be consistent under endogeneity, one can assess the potential magnitude of the bias from using simple OLS. Perform a Hausman-type specification test, comparing the coefficients from the super-consistent CPR estimator against those from a simple OLS regression of the levels. A significant difference would indicate that the endogeneity bias in OLS is material and that the use of the modified estimator was justified [[402](https://re.public.polimi.it/retrieve/handle/11311/1214370/737109/econometrics-10-00021-v2_Interview%20Johansen.pdf)].

3.  **Multicollinearity Re-evaluation:** Recalculate the VIFs and condition numbers for the complete set of regressors included in the final chosen model (e.g., the DOLS or FM-OLS model). This step ensures that the mitigation strategies employed were sufficient and that the final design matrix is numerically stable enough for reliable inference [[81](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/280303305_Revisiting_the_condition_number_and_red_indicator_in_ridge_regression)].

By following this structured empirical roadmap, a researcher can navigate the complexities of interactive cointegration, moving from initial hypothesis testing to robust estimation and final validation in a systematic and defensible manner.

## Addressing State-Dependent vs. Time-Varying Parameter Elasticities

A critical distinction in the analysis of the elasticity parameter `d` is between two different economic interpretations: a **state-dependent elasticity** and a **time-varying parameter elasticity**. Understanding this difference is paramount, as it dictates the appropriateness of the static interactive cointegration model and informs the choice of subsequent analytical methods.

The **state-dependent elasticity** interpretation aligns with the standard application of the interactive model. In this scenario, the coefficient `d` is assumed to be a constant throughout the sample period. However, the marginal effect of a change in $x_t$ on $y_t$ is not constant; it is given by the expression $b + d \cdot y_{t-1}$. Since $y_{t-1}$ is a stochastic process that evolves over time, this marginal effect also varies stochastically. The elasticity is "state-dependent" because its magnitude depends on the realized state of the economy, represented by the value of the lagged dependent variable $y_{t-1}$. The interactive cointegration model, when estimated with a constant `d`, is precisely designed to capture this phenomenon. The estimated `d` from the CPR framework directly identifies the sensitivity of the response to $x_t$ across different states of the system. This is a form of non-linearity driven by the dynamics of the system itself, not by a change in the underlying relationship.

In contrast, a **time-varying parameter (TVP) elasticity** implies that the fundamental relationship between the variables is not stable over time. Here, the coefficient `d` is not a constant but a parameter that evolves over time, denoted as $d_t$. The marginal effect becomes $b + d_t \cdot y_{t-1}$, where $d_t$ can drift or jump due to structural changes, policy shifts, technological advancements, or other exogenous shocks. This scenario fundamentally violates the core assumption of a fixed cointegrating vector, which posits a stable long-run equilibrium relationship. If the cointegrating vector is changing over time, the very notion of a single, persistent long-run relationship becomes questionable.

When the true Data Generating Process (DGP) features a time-varying $d_t$, the static interactive cointegration model is misspecified. Standard residual-based cointegration tests, which assume a constant cointegrating vector, may fail to detect a relationship that exists only over certain sub-periods or may incorrectly suggest cointegration where the relationship is unstable [[349](https://users.ssc.wisc.edu/~behansen/papers/jbes_92.pdf), [380](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/ecmode/v19y2002i5p783-799.html)]. The estimates of `d` from a static model would be meaningless, as they would only represent some time-averaged or ineffective approximation of a truly dynamic process.

To address the possibility of a time-varying parameter, several advanced econometric methodologies can be employed:
1.  **Time-Varying Coefficient (TVC) Models:** These models explicitly allow regression coefficients to change over time. The coefficient $d_t$ can be modeled as a random walk, $d_t = d_{t-1} + \eta_t$, where $\eta_t$ is a white noise error term [[356](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/46544661_Time_Varying_Cointegration)]. This can be estimated using a Kalman filter within a state-space framework, or through local likelihood methods. Such models are designed to capture smooth evolution in the relationship [[127](https://www.monash.edu/business/ebs/research/publications/ebs/wp11-17.pdf), [255](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/152725/1/LPG_JE_2019_October_24.pdf)].
2.  **Functional-Coefficient Cointegration Models:** Proposed by researchers like Cai, Li, and Park (2001), these models allow coefficients to be functions of another variable, known as a threshold variable [[358](https://tuengr.com/V12/12A4Q.pdf)]. For example, the coefficient `d` could be a smooth function of time or another observable variable, capturing regime-switching behavior without requiring discrete thresholds. This approach provides a flexible way to model non-linearities that evolve smoothly [[358](https://tuengr.com/V12/12A4Q.pdf)].
3.  **Rolling-Window Estimation:** A simpler, non-parametric approach is to repeatedly estimate the interactive cointegration model over a rolling window of the data. By tracking the estimated `d` coefficient over time, one can visually inspect for evidence of instability or structural breaks. While computationally straightforward, this method has lower efficiency compared to formal parametric models [[359](https://www.academia.edu/169015023/Detecting_the_Best_Performing_Time_Variant_Cointegration_Test_Using_the_Consumption_Function)].
4.  **Bootstrap-Based Instability Tests:** One can employ bootstrap methods to develop tests for parameter stability within a cointegration framework. These tests can formally assess the null hypothesis of a constant cointegrating vector against the alternative of parameter variation [[309](https://journal.r-project.org/articles/RJ-2024-003/), [355](https://scispace.com/pdf/bootstrap-tests-for-time-varying-cointegration-4ybzja9kt4.pdf)].

The choice of methodology depends on the specific characteristics of the data and the nature of the hypothesized time-variation. If preliminary analysis of the static model reveals significant residual autocorrelation or if economic theory suggests a plausible source of instability, moving to a TVP framework is a necessary and logical next step. The ability to distinguish between a state-dependent effect and a truly time-varying parameter is therefore a crucial aspect of a thorough econometric investigation.

## Synthesis and Final Recommendations

This deep methodological research provides a comprehensive guide for applying super-consistent cointegration estimators to an interactive time series model featuring a time-varying elasticity parameter. The analysis reveals that while the model presents significant econometric challenges, particularly the "I(2) trap" arising from the interaction of two I(1) variables, modern econometric theory offers robust solutions.

The central finding is that standard super-consistent estimators like FM-OLS, DOLS, and IM-OLS cannot be applied naively. Their theoretical underpinnings require adaptation to the specific context of Cointegrating Polynomial Regressions (CPRs), which are designed to handle models with mixed-order integrated regressors. Fortunately, the literature provides direct extensions of all three estimators for this purpose, enabling consistent estimation of the model parameters, including the crucial elasticity coefficient `d` [[145](https://www.econstor.eu/bitstream/10419/68510/1/734405871.pdf), [313](https://d-nb.info/1138836575/34), [338](https://core.ac.uk/download/pdf/189452423.pdf)].

Among the available estimators, **Integrated Modified OLS (IM-OLS)** emerges as a particularly compelling choice for this interactive model. Its primary advantage is its conceptual elegance and computational simplicity; it avoids the two most challenging aspects of the other estimators: the difficult and subjective estimation of long-run covariance matrices required by FM-OLS, and the proliferation of regressors from leads and lags of the interaction term that plagues DOLS [[155](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/254423186_Integrated_Modified_OLS_Estimation_and_Fixed-b_Inference_for_Cointegrating_Regressions), [170](https://ideas.repec.org/p/ihs/ihswps/53.html)]. By relying on an integration transformation, IM-OLS efficiently handles the serial correlation and endogeneity issues inherent in the model without introducing additional sources of complexity or potential bias.

However, the practical implementation of the interactive model is fraught with challenges, most notably severe multicollinearity. The inclusion of levels, lags, differences, and the interaction term creates a regressor matrix that is almost certain to be ill-conditioned. A proactive and rigorous approach to this problem is non-negotiable. This involves first diagnosing the extent of multicollinearity using metrics like the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) and condition numbers, and then applying a mitigation strategy. Within the traditional econometric toolkit, **orthogonalization** of the interaction term against its constituent variables is the most statistically principled method [[285](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/258136259_Orthogonalizing_Through_Residual_Centering), [288](https://www.statalist.org/forums/forum/general-stata-discussion/general/501263-stata-tip-118-orthogonalizing-powered-and-product-terms-using-residual-centering)]. As a modern alternative, **ridge regression** offers a robust way to stabilize coefficient estimates by introducing a bias-variance trade-off, making it highly suitable for ill-conditioned systems [[417](https://journal.pandawainstitute.com/index.php/jmans/article/view/251), [421](https://www.ibm.com/think/topics/ridge-regression)].

Finally, the research underscores the importance of correctly interpreting the estimated elasticity parameter `d`. The static interactive model is best suited for identifying a **state-dependent elasticity**, where `d` is constant but the effect varies with the state of the system (i.e., the value of $y_{t-1}$). If evidence suggests that the underlying relationship is not stable, and `d` itself is time-varying, the model is misspecified. In such cases, one must move to more advanced frameworks like Time-Varying Coefficient (TVC) models or functional-coefficient cointegration to capture the evolving nature of the relationship.

In conclusion, the path forward for a researcher aiming to estimate this interactive cointegrating regression is clear. First, embrace the Polynomial Cointegration framework as the necessary theoretical foundation. Second, consider IM-OLS as the preferred estimation method due to its robustness and simplicity. Third, implement a rigorous strategy to diagnose and mitigate severe multicollinearity, using orthogonalization or regularization as needed. Fourth, interpret the coefficient `d` cautiously as a state-dependent elasticity, and be prepared to employ more dynamic modeling techniques if evidence of parameter instability is found. By adhering to this methodologically sound approach, one can navigate the complexities of the model and produce credible, insightful empirical results.
