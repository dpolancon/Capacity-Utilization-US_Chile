## Executive Summary

This report outlines the empirical progress made on Chapter 2 over the recent drafting cycle. The primary objective was to ground the theoretical model of productive capacity in a century of U.S. macroeconomic data (1925–2025). I have successfully completed three major milestones:
1. **Estimated the baseline single-aggregate capital model (Specification A)** using IM-OLS, confirming the state-dependent profit squeeze effect ($\hat{\theta}_1 = -8.40^*$).
2. **Reconstructed the absolute level of capacity utilization** ($\hat{\mu}_t$) by anchoring the series to the 1973 FRB peak benchmark ($\mu_{1973} = 1.00$).
3. **Identified the long-run political economy payoff**: the U.S. economy has operated strictly within a $\theta_t < 1$ regime of capital overaccumulation across the entire post-1929 era.

Below is a summary of the methodology, key findings, and immediate next steps for transitioning to Specification B.

## 1. Analytical Setup and Historical Grounding

In Chapter 2’s framework, productive capacity ($Y^p_t$) is not a neutral technical given; it is the historical result of accumulation conditioned by class distribution. 

### Sectoral Boundary Architecture:
- **Observed Aggregate Output ($Y_t$)**: Always corresponds to *Total Corporate Gross Value Added* ($Y_{\text{CORP}}$) in NIPA accounts, capturing the overall output scale of corporate capitalist production.
- **Productive Capital Stock ($K^{\text{cap}}$) & Productive Capacity ($Y^p_t$)**: Derived strictly from *Non-Financial Corporate* (NFC) fixed assets ($K_{\text{NFC}}^{\text{ME}} + K_{\text{NFC}}^{\text{NRC}}$). Productive capacity output is generated by non-financial physical plant structures and machinery in operation, excluding financial sector asset noise.

While extensive plant structures ($K^{\text{NRC}}$) and intensive machinery ($K^{\text{ME}}$) drive physical output capacity, the *choice of technique* is governed by distributional conflict. Sunk investment horizons mean structures evolve independently of short-run profit squeezes ($\frac{\partial K^{\text{NRC}}}{\partial \omega} = 0$), whereas tactical machinery investment responds directly to lagged wage shares ($\omega_{t-1}$).

Before running parametric estimations, I mapped raw historical trajectories to ground the data in concrete institutional transitions.

A non-parametric LOESS trend of log aggregate capital ($\ln K^{\text{cap}}$) revealed five structural inflections (zero-crossings of the 2nd derivative, $\frac{d^2 \hat{k}^{\text{cap}}_t}{dt^2} = 0$). ## 2. Estimation Strategy and the Profit Squeeze Effect

To test the baseline model, I estimated the capacity equation via Integrated Modified OLS (IM-OLS; Vogelsang \& Wagner 2014), which ensures cointegration residuals are stationary ($u_t \sim I(0)$):
$$y_t = \alpha + \theta_0 \, k_t^{\text{cap}} + \phi \, \omega_t + \theta_1 \, (k_t^{\text{cap}} \cdot \omega_{t-1}) + \boldsymbol{\lambda}' \mathbf{D}_t + u_t$$
where $y_t = \ln Y_t$ (Corporate GVA) and $k_t^{\text{cap}} = \ln (K_{t, \text{NFC}}^{\text{ME}} + K_{t, \text{NFC}}^{\text{NRC}})$. The total state-dependent capital-capacity transformation elasticity is defined as:
$$\theta_t(\omega_{t-1}) = \theta_0 + \theta_1 \, \omega_{t-1}$$
preserving $\theta$ from Chapter 1 as the capital-capacity elasticity, where $\theta_0$ measures baseline scale elasticity, $\theta_1$ captures state-dependent interaction elasticity, $\phi$ measures direct baseline wage share effect, and $\boldsymbol{\lambda}' \mathbf{D}_t$ vectors structural step-dummy controls.

### Methodological Clarification on Normalization
To prevent linguistic confusion between historical *accumulation regimes* and econometric transformations, I refer to variable centering choices as **Variable Normalization Schemes**:
- **Uncentered Raw Levels**: Uses raw log levels $k_t^{\text{cap}}$ and $\omega_{t-1}$. Main effect $\hat{\theta}_0$ evaluates scale elasticity when $\omega_{t-1} = 0$, extrapolating to an unobservable zero-wage state.
- **Initial-State Normalization**: Normalizes regressors to initial 1929 values, $(k_t - k_1)$ and $(\omega_{t-1} - \omega_1)$, anchoring $\hat{\theta}_0$ to the initial historical benchmark.
- **Sample-Mean Normalization**: Normalizes regressors around sample averages, $(k_t - \bar{k})$ and $(\omega_{t-1} - \bar{\omega})$, anchoring $\hat{\theta}_0$ to average distributive conditions.

**Analytical Invariance:** Crucially, under linear shift transformations, the interaction coefficient $\hat{\theta}_1$ and its Fixed-$b$ HAR $t$-statistic are *identically invariant*. Normalization only clarifies the economic reference point of lower-order main effects ($\hat{\theta}_0$ and $\hat{\phi}$).

### Core Empirical Finding
Across the full century sample, controlling for historical step-dummies, the interaction parameter is robustly estimated at **$\hat{\theta}_1 = -8.40^*$**. 

*Economic Interpretation:* If the lagged non-financial corporate wage share rises by 5 percentage points (e.g., from 0.60 to 0.65 due to a shop-floor labor squeeze), the effective elasticity of productive capacity with respect to aggregate capital declines by $8.40 \times 0.05 = 0.42$ points. Simply put: **capital accumulation produces less capacity output when wage pressure squeezes profitability.** I have verified that this result holds robustly across all corporate and Shaikh-Tonak productive labor boundaries (e.g., $\hat{\theta}_1 = -8.88^{**}$ for Shaikh-Tonak Tier 1).

## 3. Milestone: Reconstructing Capacity Utilization

A key methodological hurdle this cycle was moving from estimating the *growth trajectory* of unobserved capacity to identifying its *absolute level* to calculate utilization ($\mu_t = Y_t / Y^p_t$).

Following vault protocol D03, I anchored the level scale by declaring $t^*_{\text{US}} = 1973$ as the full-capacity reference pinch year:
$$\mu_{\text{US}, 1973} = 1.00 \quad (100\% \text{ full capacity})$$
This anchor is externally motivated, as 1973 corresponds to the maximum peak capacity utilization observation in the Federal Reserve Board (FRB) survey-based series during the post-war expansion. By calculating the calibrated level-anchoring intercept $\hat{\alpha}^* = y_{1973} - \hat{y}_{1973}^{p, \text{raw}}$, I successfully reconstructed the absolute level of productive capacity and utilization.

The resulting utilization series captures the classic macroeconomic trajectory: deep capacity underutilization during the Great Depression ($\mu \approx 62\%$), peak wartime conversion ($\mu \approx 122\%$), sustained Golden Age utilization ($\mu \approx 85\text{--}100\%$), the severe stagflation trough of 1982 ($\mu \approx 50\%$), and persistent post-GFC sub-80\% stagnation.

## 4. Theoretical Payoff: The $\theta < 1$ Overaccumulation Regime

The most significant theoretical insight to emerge from this empirical work is the behavior of the state-dependent transformation elasticity, $\theta_t = \frac{\partial y_t^p}{\partial k_t^{\text{cap}}} = \theta_0 + \theta_1 \, \omega_{t-1}$.

### Theoretical Regime Threshold Classification
We distinguish two fundamental reproduction regimes based on the threshold value of $\theta_t$:
1. **Productive Overcapacity Regime ($\theta_t > 1$)**: Capital accumulation expands physical capacity faster than the capital stock itself ($g_{Y^p} > g_{K^{\text{cap}}}$). This requires continuous, exponential demand expansion to avoid structural overcapacity.
2. **Capital Overaccumulation Regime ($\theta_t < 1$)**: Capital accumulation outpaces physical capacity creation ($g_{Y^p} < g_{K^{\text{cap}}}$). Capitalists must invest progressively more capital per unit of capacity created ($1/\theta_t > 1$).

### The Empirical Reality: Lock-In Within $\theta_t < 1$
The uncentered marginal elasticity estimates reveal that the U.S. economy has operated **strictly within the $\theta_t < 1$ regime** across the entire post-1929 sample. Marginal elasticity ranges between $\theta_{2024} = 0.686$ and $\theta_{1933} = 0.801$, with a sample mean of $\bar{\theta} = 0.745$. At no point in modern U.S. history does $\theta_t$ cross above 1.

![Figure 6: Transformation Elasticity Time Series](file:///c:/ReposGitHub/Capacity-Utilization-US_Chile/output/figures/fig_transformation_elasticity_theta_timeseries.png)

### The Dual Structural Constraint
This empirical lock-in delivers a crucial political economy conclusion for Chapter 2: 21st-century U.S. capitalism operates under a **dual structural constraint**. On the supply side, there is a secular erosion of the capacity payoff (an overaccumulation bottleneck where $\theta_t \approx 0.686 < 1$, requiring 1.46 units of capital per unit of capacity created). On the demand side, there is a realization shortfall ($\mu_t < 80\%$) produced by neoliberal wage repression. Even when post-1980 restructuring successfully suppressed worker compensation ($\omega$ falling from 0.64 to 0.56), it failed to restore high capacity elasticity; instead, share-buyback financialization pushed $\theta_t$ to its historic low.

## 5. Immediate Next Steps: Transition to Specification B

The empirical audit for Specification A is complete and yields a clear conclusion: distribution fundamentally constrains capacity formation, and the U.S. economy operates locked within the $\theta_t < 1$ regime of capital overaccumulation.

However, the baseline single-aggregate model ($K^{\text{cap}} = K^{\text{ME}} + K^{\text{NRC}}$) imposes an arbitrary mathematical restriction. It forces the mechanization choice ($\tau_t = k^{\text{ME}}_t - k^{\text{NRC}}_t$) to expand in exact proportion to plant scale, masking the endogenous *choice of technique*.

### Immediate Next Steps for Chapter 2
To capture how capitalists alter the organic composition of capital in response to distributional shocks without aggregation bias, I am now advancing to **Specification B**. This specification disaggregates the extensive plant footprint ($k^{\text{NRC}}$) from the intensive mechanization choice ($\tau_t$). I expect to have the initial estimates for Specification B ready for the next committee review.
