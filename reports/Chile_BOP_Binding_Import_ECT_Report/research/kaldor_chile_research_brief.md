# Kaldor, import requirements, and the Chilean balance-of-payments constraint

## Research question

Can the import error-correction term in a Chilean import-demand system be interpreted as evidence that the balance-of-payments constraint has become binding, and what role do the real exchange rate, non-reinvested surplus, and machinery accumulation play in that mechanism?

## Bottom line

The literature supports a structural mechanism but not the identification shortcut originally proposed. Kaldorian growth theory makes manufacturing expansion productivity-enhancing through increasing returns and cumulative causation; Thirlwall's balance-of-payments closure makes sustained output growth conditional on the foreign exchange generated by exports and financing relative to the income elasticity of imports. Chilean historical evidence supplies the connecting fact: industrialization raised demand for imported machinery and intermediate inputs while export stagnation periodically restricted the foreign exchange needed to sustain that transformation. This mechanism makes an import disequilibrium term economically informative, but it does not make the term a sufficient classifier of balance-of-payments crises.

The implemented evidence therefore separates three objects. The first is a long-run import relation, which must pass the integration-order and cointegration gates. The second is an observed real-exchange-rate regime, which may condition adjustment only if a formal threshold test rejects linearity. The third is a balance-of-payments state, which requires agreement between model-based import pressure and observed external indicators. The error-correction term enters the third object as one signal, not as the latent state by construction.

## Mechanism map

1. **Productive transformation.** Machinery accumulation can raise productive capacity and support manufacturing growth, while manufacturing growth can generate increasing returns and faster productivity growth. This is the Kaldorian mechanism, not a claim that every machinery coefficient is automatically causal.
2. **Import requirement.** For a late-industrializing economy, machinery and intermediate inputs can have low short-run domestic substitutability. Expansion therefore raises the demand for imports needed to reproduce and transform productive capacity.
3. **Foreign-exchange closure.** Imports must be financed by exports, transfers, reserves, or sustainable capital inflows. When export growth and financing fail to accommodate the import requirement, output and accumulation must adjust.
4. **Distribution and surplus disposition.** A larger non-reinvested surplus can reduce domestic accumulation without necessarily relaxing the external constraint. Its sign in an import relation is therefore an empirical matter that depends on whether retained surplus finances domestic investment, foreign assets, consumption imports, or other uses.
5. **Real exchange rate.** A real depreciation may compress imports, redirect demand, or support tradables, but its effect depends on pass-through, import dependence, and productive capacity. The RER is consequently an observed conditioning variable, not an automatic crisis label.
6. **Binding state.** A negative normalized import error-correction term can indicate imports below the level implied by the estimated long-run relation. It becomes evidence of a binding external constraint only when the relation is statistically admissible and the signal agrees with observed balance-of-payments stress.

## Chilean evidence

The Banco Central de Chile's centennial history identifies the critical historical sequence directly. CORFO-led industrialization increased imports of capital and intermediate goods from the late 1940s; weak export performance then left inadequate foreign exchange, culminating in the 1961 balance-of-payments crisis and abandonment of the fixed exchange rate. This evidence validates the structural question posed by the model, but it also supplies a hard external benchmark: a state classifier that misses 1961 cannot be treated as a definitive chronology.

Álvarez and Fuentes show why the import channel cannot be reduced to consumption leakage. Their manufacturing analysis emphasizes that developing countries import capital and intermediate goods embodying technology and that domestic substitutes may be limited. Their post-reform results also challenge a simple narrative in which liberalization only destroyed Chilean manufacturing. The relevant conclusion is narrower: imported inputs and capital can raise productivity, while trade reform can simultaneously alter industrial composition, import penetration, and exposure to external financing.

Albala-Bertrand finds that Chile's 1986–1996 production structure remained weakly articulated and that import penetration displaced domestic interindustry linkages. Palma places the wider Latin American shift inside a premature-deindustrialization and Dutch-disease debate. Solimano and Zapata-Román document Chile's continued copper dependence, structural transformation, high inequality, and the crisis breaks surrounding the post-1973 model. Together, these studies make the post-1974 sample a distinct political-economic regime rather than a larger continuation of the pre-1973 system.

## What the literature does not establish

- Kaldor's laws do not identify the cointegrating rank or integration order of Chilean machinery capital.
- Thirlwall's law does not imply that every import residual is a balance-of-payments crisis.
- A real exchange-rate threshold does not exist merely because the sample contains devaluations or crises.
- Panel evidence for Latin America does not establish a Chile-specific causal coefficient.
- The 1980–2019 Chilean balance-of-payments-constrained-growth study cannot adjudicate the pre-1973 mechanism or the 1961 crisis.
- Historical agreement between a model signal and a known episode is validation evidence, not proof of a uniquely identified latent state.

## Implications for the implemented report

The report should present the machinery specification as the motivating structural model and then enforce the stochastic gate. Because gross machinery capital is not admitted as an \(I(1)\) variable in either split sample, its coefficient and its machinery-conditioned import ECT remain forensic results rather than promoted estimates. The reduced admissible systems can still test whether import adjustment changes across observed RER regimes, but they cannot answer the original machinery question.

The balance-of-payments index should therefore be described as a screening and triangulation device. Confirmed binding requires both model pressure and an observed external-sector signal; ECT-only and observed-only years remain separate categories. The Banco Central's 1961 chronology must appear beside the model classification so that readers can see the false-negative risk rather than infer accuracy from internally generated dates.
